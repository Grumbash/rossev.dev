I joined YAGNI three weeks ago. In that time I've set up a cold email campaign, made a promo video, worked on how our agents set up a customer's environment, and merged 28 pull requests in the last week alone.

There are three of us, we meet every other day, and each call decides what I own until the next one. Some cycles that's engineering. The next one can be sales, or a video, or whatever is blocking the business that week.

## What my last two weeks looked like

On November 5 I was on lead generation. The cold email we had been sending had brought nothing, and when I looked at it, the targeting was the problem: generic lists, no reason for anyone to reply. So I rebuilt it around a conference in Berlin, with a list of the people speaking and exhibiting there and a reason to write to each of them.

On November 7 I finished a short promo video about our agents.

On November 9 Bruno was back from vacation, and the call reset our priorities: the product had to handle a simple ticket reliably before anything else. From that day I was an engineer again. On the 11th I picked up how an environment gets set up once by a person so agents can work from a copy of it. On the 16th I took over splitting big tickets into smaller ones.

Four different jobs in under two weeks, and each switch was decided on one of our calls.

## Why switching doesn't drop the work

Leaving engineering for two days used to mean leaving a half-written branch in my head, and the first morning back went to remembering where I was.

Now the agents keep working the engineering tickets while I'm on something else. When I come back, I come back to pull requests, review comments and failed runs, all written down in GitHub and Linear. Switching back costs reading. I don't have to remember anything, because nothing was left only in my head.

That's what makes a two-day cycle possible for me. If the work stopped every time I stepped away from it, I couldn't afford to step away.

## What it costs

Attention. I counted it once: over two weeks of my Claude Code sessions, I typed about ten messages for every pull request that got merged. About one in five of those was coordination: telling an agent what another one did, restating context, moving work along. Almost half were decisions.

Switching jobs every two days adds to that. The first hours of a new cycle go to catching up on what changed while I was away, and on a sales day the engineering messages still arrive. [HOW I HANDLE IT: e.g. when I check pull requests on a non-engineering day]

## What I had to unlearn

For most of my career a good day meant code that shipped. Here some of my most useful days had no code in them, like the day I rebuilt the email targeting.

I also had to get used to dropping things. When a call decides that what I started two days ago doesn't matter anymore, I close it and move on. With agents doing much of the building, an abandoned direction costs two days, and two days is cheap enough to try things.
