Your weekend AI project works. Your customers should be fucking terrified if that's your entire engineering strategy.

I love that I can now get annoyed at my keyboard, delete the RGB bloatware, and build a replacement in Rust in two hours. Did exactly that. A couple of bugs remain. Couldn't care less.

I turned on my Windows gaming PC and the lighting was flickering again. Something had taken control, something else wanted it back, and apparently I was supposed to troubleshoot this relationship. So I removed the lighting apps and built my own controller with a coding agent.

If it breaks, my keyboard goes back to looking like a gas station gaming lounge. I'll fix it whenever I feel like it.

## Software you can afford to abandon

There's already a name for this territory.

Robin Sloan wrote about [home-cooked software](https://www.robinsloan.com/notes/home-cooked-app/) in 2020: an app made for particular people, without the obligation to become a business. Geoffrey Litt's [Malleable software in the age of LLMs](https://www.geoffreylitt.com/2023/03/25/llm-end-user-programming.html) explicitly imagined one-off scripts and interfaces generated for specific tasks. Maggie Appleton later explored the same opportunity in [Home-Cooked Software and Barefoot Developers](https://maggieappleton.com/home-cooked-software).

My keyboard controller fits somewhere in there. I keep thinking of it as disposable software, although I might use it for years.

"Disposable" describes how little I have to commit to it. It doesn't need a roadmap. It doesn't need to support somebody else's keyboard. If it breaks, I can ask an agent to fix it, leave it broken until the weekend, or reinstall one of the apps I removed.

The remaining bugs are acceptable because I'm the person accepting them. Nobody else has bought a promise from me.

That changes the economics of building software. A tool can be worth making even when polishing it into a product would be a terrible use of time.

## The support didn't disappear

Then somebody does this once and decides engineering teams were mostly unnecessary overhead.

Apparently "I can fix it with another prompt" now counts as a maintenance strategy. Sure. How long can your customer wait while you figure out which prompt?

The actual fix might cost three dollars. The broken invoices, lost work, and day of downtime don't.

My maintenance cost didn't disappear. I agreed to do the maintenance whenever I feel like it. I also own the decision to do absolutely nothing about it. The consequence is a keyboard displaying the wrong colour. I'll survive.

Now imagine somebody pays me to run a service their team depends on.

"Maybe I'll fix it this weekend" becomes a much less charming operating model.

The first working version still matters. Getting there in two hours is useful. But once other people depend on it, I need answers to questions that my keyboard lets me ignore. How will I notice a failure? Can I roll back safely? What happens to work already in progress? Can I change one thing without quietly breaking three others?

A coding agent can help with all of that. I use them every day. But generating the implementation doesn't automatically establish that those questions have been answered.

## Show me the recovery

The recent Microsoft 365 outage made this distinction harder to ignore.

In the [incident updates reproduced by ENow](https://www.enowsoftware.com/office-365-monitoring-outages/microsoft-service-health-updates-for-microsoft-outages-mo1550821-and-ex1550543), Microsoft described unsuccessful recovery attempts, unresponsive servers preventing validation, and further attempts to isolate infrastructure and reintroduce traffic.

Microsoft has [said the incident was not related to AI or agents](https://www.kuow.org/technology/2026-11-13/microsoft-says-outage-not-caused-by-ai-or-cyber-attack). Those updates don't establish that AI caused it, and I can't honestly make that claim.

What they do make me want to ask is: when companies celebrate how much faster AI lets them change software, where is the corresponding evidence about their ability to understand and recover it?

I'd like to hear about a failure caught before deployment. A rollback that worked under real conditions. A change that somebody could explain six months after the agent wrote it.

Those would tell me more about delivery than the percentage of code generated by a model.

My keyboard controller is a good outcome. Two hours, a narrow problem solved, a few imperfections I'm happy to live with. We should have more software like that.

We should also be explicit about when other people are being asked to live with those imperfections.

I want coding agents doing more of the work. I also want us to stop pretending that making software cheap makes failure cheap.

You can generate the app in two hours. The customer is still buying the next two years.
